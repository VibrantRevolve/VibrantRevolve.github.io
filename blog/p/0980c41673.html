Tracing the recruitment trap behind Russia’s war

No justice for Bangladeshi workers who are recruited into Russia’s war

Originally published on Global Voices

War-wounded Bangladeshis in Russia plead to return home. Screenshot from a YouTube report by Ekhon TV. Fair use.

Maksudur Rahman was 31 when a labor recruiter arrived in his hometown in Lakshmipur district, southeastern Bangladesh, with a job offer: becoming a janitor in Russia. Nearly every family in the district has someone working overseas, so the pitch landed. According to an international investigation , he borrowed money and paid the broker BDT 1.2 million (about USD 9,800). For a household like his, that is a fortune, but the promised wages made the debt look manageable. He signed up and flew out.

What followed had nothing to do with brooms. Within weeks, he was on the front lines of Russia’s war in Ukraine . He and three other Bangladeshi workers were handed a paper written in Russian — a language they did not understand — and told to sign. Then came an army camp, where recruits were trained in drone tactics, medical evacuation, and heavy weapons. When Rahman protested, a Russian commander replied through a translation app that his own agent had sent him there. Workers in his group were threatened with ten-year jail terms and beaten , he said.

His story is a case study in how this insidious war pipeline operates: a local agent, a borrowed fortune, a contract nobody could read, and a war that was never part of the bargain. But to understand how a man from Lakshmipur ended up in that camp, it’s important to look at the realities in Russia and what is driving these recruitment traps.

A war that is running out of men

Russia has a manpower problem, and the figures are hard to wave away. Enlistment into contract service dropped about 20 percent in the first quarter of 2026 compared with the same period a year earlier, according to international reporting relayed by Ukrainian outlets. This deficit persists even though Moscow is offering signing packages of up to USD 80,000 and debt write-offs of up to USD 140,000.

Meanwhile, BBC and Mediazona journalists estimate there have been at least 300,000 Russian deaths since the war began on February 24, 2022, based on local obituaries . Other Western intelligence estimates put Russian deaths near 500,000, with monthly casualties of 30,000 to 35,000.

One estimate says the country needs another 1.5 million workers to stabilize its labor market, and officials themselves expect a shortfall of 3.1 million people by 2030. Because stricter migration rules have also pushed out roughly a million temporary workers, the army and the factories now compete for the same shrinking labor pool.

While Russia has not dared to implement another full-scale military draft as it did in September 2022 — a move that was highly damaging to the regime and local support — officials are doing everything they can to buy recruits or force those who are vulnerable, elderly, or simply went to the draft station to register to go to the front lines .

Given these manpower challenges, Moscow has looked outward. It has already used tens of thousands of prisoners and three waves of North Korean troops, yet that wasn’t enough.

A UK defense assessment, summarized by a Ukrainian outlet, says the supply of convict recruits is probably exhausted, and Russia is widening its search across the Global South to avoid another mobilization at home.

Ukrainian data counts nearly 29,000 foreigners who have signed contracts since 2022. One report says the army wants at least 18,500 more in 2026, naming Bangladesh among its top targets.

Rights groups see a pattern behind these numbers. The International Federation for Human Rights described an organized pipeline aimed at undocumented migrants, detainees, precarious workers, and foreign students across three continents, and Amnesty International accused Moscow last month of deceptive, coercive, and exploitative recruitment.

Who is accepting this offer?

Within South Asia, this recruitment scheme goes beyond just Bangladesh. After Sri Lanka’s economic collapse in 2022 and Nepal’s recession, wages of at least USD 550 a month in Russia looked like a way out, according to analysis from the Observer Research Foundation ( ORF ). Nepal’s foreign minister admitted in early 2024 that more than 200 Nepali citizens had been recruited. Kathmandu later suspended work permits for Russia after numerous Nepali citizens died on the front lines in Ukraine. Sri Lanka, for its part, saw an estimated 600 to 700 former soldiers recruited.

Why, then, would anyone take the risk? Money comes first. One welder, according to a report on survivors’ accounts, was offered a USD 20,990 signing bonus, a salary of USD 2,664 per month, and a shot at citizenship. For families buried in debt, those numbers can drown out the danger.

But money isn’t the whole answer. Some Bangladeshis enlisted knowingly were misled about the role, the reporting noted , while others lost a visa-linked job and found a recruiter waiting with a better-sounding contract. Some never aimed for Russia at all: One prisoner said an Uzbek broker had promised him residence in Italy . Once inside, the choice largely disappears, as recruits were presented with contracts in Russian, with those who hesitated facing threats and violence, according to Fortify Rights and Truth Hounds . Another report described passports and phones seized on arrival.

The legal door and the missing agreement

Russia was never a typical destination for Bangladeshi workers. This labor force first entered the country via shipyards, where skilled hands were needed as the war strained the domestic labor supply. Initially, around 56 workers were sent there. Then the flow increased. According to the data of the Refugee and Migratory Movements Research Unit ( RMMRU ), 993 Bangladeshis went in 2024 and 4,663 in 2025. The Bureau of Manpower Employment and Training ( BMET ) records 8,247 departures between 2024 and June 2026. The state-run Bangladesh Overseas Employment and Services Limited ( BOESL ) sent just 426 among them, and those workers are reported safe ; the private market carried the rest.

However, even with this sizable flow of workers, no signed labor agreement seems to exist between the two countries. During Foreign Minister Khalilur Rahman’s June 2026 visit to Moscow, both sides merely urged early conclusion of pending accords, including a human resource agreement and a readmission agreement. Russia has agreed to fast-track a proposal for 100,000 Bangladeshi workers within a year. A state minister even spoke of 300,000 to 400,000 workers over five years. Meanwhile, Dhaka’s embassy in Moscow has asked Russia to bar Bangladeshis from military service.

The lack of a formal agreement leaves Bangladeshi workers exposed and vulnerable to scams and exploitation.

Fraud also shows up outside the war. In January 2026, 35 workers returned from Russia after being terminated, saying they had each paid about BDT 700,000 (roughly USD 5,651).

Agents, agencies, and the price of a seat

The route from village to barracks runs through ordinary offices. Bangladesh’s Special Response Battalion (SRB) said 30 men who flew to Russia in May 2026 went through three manpower firms, Jabal-e-Noor International, RS International, and TS Group. Officers arrested two alleged ringleaders in mid-October and say a Russian man and a Kyrgyz woman were part of the network, though these allegations remain unconfirmed. The expatriates’ welfare minister told Parliament that the men left with BMET clearance through licensed agencies, which shows that paperwork alone proves nothing.

Once the men landed, the script repeated. Their passports and phones were taken. After 21 days of training, they went to the front. Investigators said they were used as bait to expose enemy positions to drones.

Counting the dead without a count

Fortify Rights and Truth Hounds identified at least 104 Bangladeshis recruited as of February 2026 and at least 34 killed, based on Ukrainian information, and warned that the real toll is probably higher. But on October 14, 2026, the SRB said 20 of the same 30 were dead, with 17 killed by a drone strike on their first day at the front and six still fighting, local news reports said.

These figures can’t simply be added together, because the overlap is unknown and the sources differ: police, rights groups, and Kyiv. Those families, meanwhile, wait without bodies.

Rights groups have urged Dhaka to dismantle the networks and Ukraine to screen captured foreign fighters for signs of trafficking. Yet families still ask for rescue, and the same daily’s editors have pleaded with the government to start bringing the stranded men home.

No Justice for the victims

Back to Maksudur Rahman: He got out, and today he sits at home in Lakshmipur, holding up a Russian military dog tag as proof of where he has been. Others from the same corner of Bangladesh haven’t come home. Sajjad, 20, was killed after being taken to fight, and his father, Mohammed Siraj, now holds a photograph of where his son used to be. For Siraj, justice is not an abstraction. It is a name on a charge sheet, a body to bury, and an answer to the question of who sent his son to war.

In the village, families confronted the recruiting agents and demanded to know why their loved ones were being trained for war. Their questions belong in a courtroom, not only in a village courtyard. Until the agents who sold these jobs and the system that cleared their paperwork are made to answer, the families will keep carrying a debt that is more than money.

Rahman’s loan was real, the war was real, and the janitor’s job never existed. A man who left home to earn a living came back carrying a soldier’s dog tag. And no justice in sight.
